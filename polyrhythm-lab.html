Polyrhythm Lab

Any number against any other. Stack two, three, or four layers of 2 to 16 notes, split a note, rest a note, and press play. The page shows every note as a bubble and works out the math, the counting, and the notation for whatever you build.

3 : 2

A polyrhythm is two or more streams of evenly spaced notes sharing one span of time. Three against two, five against four, seven against three against five: they all work the same way, and once you can calculate one you can calculate any of them.

Each bubble is as wide as its note is long. Every layer gets a row, and the bottom row puts them all on one line so you can see the rhythm your hands make together. Layers that land together share a striped bubble.

Set the numbers with the plus and minus buttons, pick which layer is the beat, and press play. Then choose what a tap does: hear a note, split it into two to seven smaller notes, or turn it into a rest. That is enough to build nested rhythms like the one from measure 21 of Rebonds a.

Small counts ticks the subdivision you would count out loud. Take turns plays a cycle, then leaves a cycle for you. Each layer sounds from its own side, so headphones help.

      Every pair up to sixteen

      Every way to pair two different numbers from 2 to 16. True polyrhythms are in color; smaller ones repeated are in outline.

      Each row is the larger number. Tap a pair to put it on the stage, with the smaller number as the beat. Five against three and three against five are the same rhythm felt from two sides, so each pair appears once.

      How to calculate any polyrhythm

      Every rhythm on this page, from three against two to a nested Xenakis beat, comes out of the same ten moves. All it takes is multiplying, dividing, and the lowest common multiple.

      1. Name the numbers

        A polyrhythm is written as a ratio, like 5:3, and read “five against three”: five even notes in the same time as three even notes, starting together. Decide which stream is the beat. The tempo belongs to it, and the other stream is measured against it.

      2. Reduce

        Divide both numbers by the largest number that divides them both. Six against four is three against two played twice, and you practice it as three against two. If nothing divides both, you have a true polyrhythm, and the streams meet only on the downbeat.

      3. Find the ruler

        Multiply the two numbers, then divide by the factor they share. The answer is their lowest common multiple, and it is the number of equal marks one cycle needs to hold both streams. For five against three, 5 × 3 = 15 marks. For six against four, 6 × 4 ÷ 2 = 12 marks.

      4. Place the notes

        Divide the ruler by each number to get that stream’s step. With 15 marks, the five moves 3 marks at a time and lands on 0, 3, 6, 9, 12. The three moves 5 marks at a time and lands on 0, 5, 10.

      5. Read the composite

        Merge the two lists into one: 0, 3, 5, 6, 9, 10, 12. Subtract each mark from the next, and run the last one to the next downbeat: 3, 2, 1, 3, 1, 2, 3. That string of gaps is the rhythm your two hands make together. It always reads the same backward, so the second half of any plain polyrhythm is the first half in reverse.

      6. Put it on the clock

        One beat lasts 60 ÷ tempo seconds. Multiply by the number of beats in the cycle, then divide by the ruler to get one mark. At 60 beats per minute with the three as the beat, the cycle lasts 3 seconds and one mark lasts 200 milliseconds, so the five plays every 600. The other stream’s metronome marking is tempo × its number ÷ the beat’s number: 60 × 5 ÷ 3 = 100.

      7. Count it

        Turn the ruler into counting. Divide every beat into as many counts as the other stream has notes, then play that stream on every Nth count, where N is the number of beats. For five against three, count each of the three beats in five and play every third count: 1 and 4, then 2 and 5, then 3. Then flip it: count five beats in three and play every fifth count. Written out, that is a stream of tuplets with accents, and it is the fastest way to get any polyrhythm into your hands.

      8. Add layers

        A third or fourth stream changes nothing but the ruler, which becomes the lowest common multiple of all the numbers. Three against four against five needs 60 marks: the three steps by 20, the four by 15, and the five by 12.

      9. Nest

        When one note of a stream is split into smaller notes, that note needs finer marks: multiply the stream’s number by the split. The ruler becomes the lowest common multiple of every one of those products. In measure 21 of Xenakis’s Rebonds a, five notes run against three sixteenths that are split into two, three, and two. The products are 5, 3 × 2 = 6, and 3 × 3 = 9, and their lowest common multiple is 90. The five steps by 18, the plain thirty-seconds by 15, and the triplet by 10. The Polyrhythm page takes that beat apart one sixteenth at a time.

      10. Hear the ratio as pitch

        Speed a polyrhythm up until the notes blur and it turns into a musical interval, because pitch is rhythm heard very fast. Two against one becomes an octave, three against two a perfect fifth, four against three a perfect fourth, five against four a major third. Four against five against six is a major chord. Build a rhythm and press Hear it as harmony to check.

      Then go find one in the music

      Polyrhythms live in real pieces, not in tables. The Polyrhythm page takes one beat of Xenakis apart with the same bubbles, and the rudiments, scales, and modes pages are where the hands that play them get built.
