The story so far

Twenty-five years behind the drums, told in photographs, concert posters, and video: from Southern California and Reno to a home studio in Hermiston, Oregon, through four music schools in three states, to the concert stage at Angelo State.

{{keys}}

1983–1998 · California, Nevada, Oregon

Three hometowns

Trent Shuey was born in North Hollywood, California, in 1983, and North Hollywood was home first. Then came Thousand Oaks, in the hills northwest of Los Angeles; then Reno, Nevada; and then, in his teens, Hermiston, a farm town on the Columbia River in eastern Oregon.

His father, Paul, was a radiographer who worked in Southern California, Nevada, and finally at Good Shepherd Hospital in Hermiston, and he was a backpacker, a fisherman, and a camper. The family camper and the rivers of the West are the first memories in this story, and they are the reason Shuey Hikes exists at the other end of it.

Each place kept a claim on him. He would go back to Reno for graduate school, and Hermiston is still where his family is anchored.

2000–2001 · Hermiston

It starts in a studio

By the time he was out of high school the local paper had taken notice. “A different drummer… the man for all styles,” the Hermiston Herald wrote in 2000, and the phrase has followed him since.

The photo is from the room where the drumming got serious: a blue drum set under a ceiling of cymbals, microphones set up, friends plugging in guitars. Most of what came later, from jazz clubs to orchestra pits to marching fields, can be traced back to afternoons in this room.

2006–2009 · Pendleton and Hermiston

Blue Mountain: teaching before finishing school

The first degree came from Blue Mountain Community College in Pendleton, forty minutes down the road from Hermiston, where he studied with Margaret Mayer and earned an Associate of Arts in 2007. He was already teaching: from 2006 to 2008 he was the percussion instructor at Hermiston High School, teaching at the school he had left only a few years before.

A year after graduating, BMCC hired him back. As Director of Percussion Studies for 2008–2009 he was building a college percussion program while still an undergraduate himself, a pattern of working several jobs at once that has never really stopped.

2007–2009 · La Grande, Oregon

Eastern Oregon: Teun Fetz and a concerto

Over the Blue Mountains in La Grande, Eastern Oregon University is where he became a percussionist in the full sense of the word. He studied with Teun Fetz, who would come to Texas fifteen years later as the guest artist for the 2022 ASU Day of Percussion, and finished a Bachelor of Music magna cum laude in 2009.

The same year he stepped in front of the Grande Ronde Symphony Orchestra as timpani soloist for the world premiere of Kvistad's Concerto for Timpani and Chamber Orchestra, his first concerto and his first world premiere. A second premiere followed at EOU, Neil Facciuto's Brugmansia for chamber ensemble, and the orchestra jobs began: principal timpani and percussion with the Grande Ronde Symphony, principal with the Inland Northwest Symphony, and concerts with the Oregon East Symphony. Along the way he also studied with Alan Keown, Matt Savage, and Jon Brill.

2009–2012 · Reno, Nevada

Nevada: jazz in the high desert

A graduate teaching assistantship took him back to Reno, the city he had lived in as a kid, and to the University of Nevada, where he studied with Andrew Heglund and Cody Remaklus and finished a Master of Music with a minor in jazz in 2012. In 2011 the Reno Jazz Festival named him Outstanding Drum Set Soloist, and the drum set side of the story, the one that started in the Hermiston studio, got its formal training here: big band, combos, and the duo with Heglund that is still on video.

Reno was also where the range widened. He played steel drums with the UNR I-Pan ensemble, spent summers on the faculty of the Lake Tahoe Music Camp, held the principal percussion and timpani chair with the Tahoe Symphony, and played with the Reno Philharmonic and the Reno Chamber Orchestra. He wrote the drumline's audition etude, taught as a graduate assistant, and recorded with the Dakota Brown Band, whose EP Find Your Way came out in 2013, and with the PS Jazz Quartet.

Heglund came to Angelo State as a guest artist in April 2019, which tells you how those years went.

2012–2015 · Urbana-Champaign

Illinois: Moersch and the marimba

Doctoral study at the University of Illinois meant years with William Moersch, one of the marimba's great commissioners, and with Ricardo Flores. Trent won the university's Edgard Varèse Award in 2013, taught as a graduate assistant in 2014–2015, worked summers at Illinois Summer Youth Music, and played with the Champaign-Urbana Symphony, with Sinfonia da Camera, and with the Illinois Modern Ensemble, whose recording came out on Bridge Records in 2015.

He was in the stands and on the field with the Marching Illini too, where the drumline gave him the nickname Snoopy. It stuck. In 2014 he gave the world premiere of Bill Ton's McAfee Variations for solo timpani at the University of South Carolina, and the solo repertoire he plays today, from Rzewski's To the Earth on four flowerpots to Xenakis's Rebonds, took shape in those years.

His dissertation became a performance guide to five marimba works commissioned by Moersch, and the recital that went with it carried three world premieres. The recordings are on SoundCloud.

2015 · San Angelo, Texas

Angelo State

In 2015 Trent joined the faculty of Angelo State University as Assistant Director of Bands and percussion instructor. He directed the Symphonic Band for his first three years, then moved to the jazz program, and he has written every battery arrangement the Ram Band Drumline has played since, more than 40 halftime shows so far.

The studio he inherited had four percussion students. It has grown to sixteen, and the drumline has gone from 8 players to more than 20. Along the way he created the Ram Horns pep band identity in 2016, founded the ASU Day of Percussion in 2017, and secured a $25,000 Rea Charitable Trust grant for new instruments. Graduates of the studio now teach and perform across Texas and beyond, with a 100 percent placement rate in their chosen fields.

2016–2017 · Building the program

Ram Horns, a concerto, and a Day of Percussion

The Ram Horns name and look date from 2016, the year he was also percussion concerto soloist with the ASU Symphony Orchestra. In January 2017 came a faculty recital in Eldon Black Recital Hall, with music by Barati, Lansky, Ling, Friedman, Volans, and Kohei, and that November the inaugural ASU Day of Percussion brought guest artist Peter Breithaupt to campus for a day of concerts, clinics, and masterclasses with the Ram Band Drumline and the Percussion Ensemble.

Every concert gets a poster

2018–2023 · Yucatán, Mexico

ÓperaMaya

Starting in 2018, summers meant the ÓperaMaya International Music Festival on Mexico's Yucatán Peninsula: teaching and coaching percussion students, playing in the festival orchestra alongside musicians from around the world, and once, in 2021, setting up timpani inside a cave for a concert. The festival was cancelled in 2020 because of the pandemic. The festival's percussion scholarship came his way in 2019 and again in 2021, and the trips are part of a performing résumé that spans the United States, Mexico, and Brazil.

{{gallery:operamaya-church,young-drummer}}

2020 · Doctor of Musical Arts

The degree, and a premiere with his name on it

The doctorate from Illinois was completed in 2020. The dissertation, A Performance Guide to the Marimba Music of Jared Beynon, Harold Farberman, James Lauth, Akmal Parwez, and Steven Rosenhaus, examines five works commissioned by Moersch: Beynon's Devil Dog, Farberman's “A” Train, Lauth's Sonata for Marimba, Parwez's Banyan Boughs, and Rosenhaus's Mission Music. Three of them had their world premieres on his recital.

The same year he gave the world premiere of William Kang's Shuey Brew, Coffee Stains, an Office, and the Spine, performing alongside the composer, and in the pandemic fall the Percussion Ensemble played Terry Riley's In C, on November 3, 2020, six years to the day before the 2026 performance at the end of this page.

2021 · Hermiston

Paul

In July 2021 Trent lost his father, Paul Thomas Shuey, a radiographer, an expert-rated chess player, and a camper and fisherman. Paul's fish painting is the reason this website looks the way it does: its colors and brushstrokes are painted under every page title. Read the memorial page.

2021–2022 · Back on stage

Big band, percussion ensemble, and the first combo nights

Concerts came back. The Big Band played a lunchtime blues set under the blue canopy, paid tribute to Sammy Nestico, and premiered El Mambo! in December 2021; the Trent Shuey Quartet EP came out the same year. The Percussion Ensemble played Magic that November and Rides, Games, and Trickery the next spring. The jazz combos started playing out: the first Jazz Combo Night at Back Beat Music Company in November 2021, then a night at the Cactus Hotel with the trumpet ensembles the following spring. His own teacher Teun Fetz came to the 2022 Day of Percussion, and August 2022 was spent on the field with the drumline.

Fort Concho

The Official Drummer

San Angelo grew up around a frontier army post, and the post is still there: Fort Concho, established in 1867, is a National Historic Landmark in the heart of the city. As the fort's Official Drummer, Trent plays an original 1867 drum at living history events, giving visitors the sound of military music on the Texas frontier. More about Fort Concho.

Conversations · with Kat Truth Bunker

He plays what he sees. She draws what she hears.

In Conversations, two artists take turns answering each other. Trent improvises on percussion in response to Kat Truth Bunker's artwork, and she draws in response to what she hears, so that each piece is shaped by listening rather than by a score. The collaboration has produced seven works so far.

2022–2023 · A home downtown

The House of FiFi DuBois

In the fall of 2022 the jazz combos moved into The House of FiFi DuBois on Chadbourne Street, and it has been home ever since: combo nights, Saturday concerts, and the open jam sessions that follow them, all under the neon and the Wild West Show sign. 2023 was a full year around it: a faculty recital at the marimba in September, the Ram Band on parade, ASU's first season with two big bands, the Big Band at the Blue Buffalo Art Gallery and Have a Swingin' Christmas at the Brooks and Bates Theatre, and the Percussion Ensemble's Fiesta Latina with the brass ensembles.

2024 · Big band history and Latin jazz

The big band finds its groove

February 2024 brought favorites from six decades of big band music to the Brooks and Bates Theatre, and the Percussion Ensemble's spring concert in April had guests from ASU's brass faculty and students. Kansas City swing and a marimba sextet

2025–2026 · Philip Glass

Two timpanists, one concerto

In September 2025 he and Dr. Lisa Rogers of Texas Tech were the soloists in Philip Glass's Concerto Fantasy for Two Timpanists with the ASU Symphony Orchestra, on a program billed as Thunderstruck that opened ASU's fall season. In February 2026 the two of them played it again with the Big Spring Symphony and its conductor, Dr. Fagner Rocha. Both performances are on video, along with a short tuning demonstration from before the concert.

Fall 2025 · FiFi's, Indianapolis, and Glimmer

A September at FiFi's, a November in Indianapolis

The jazz combos opened the fall at The House of FiFi DuBois on September 13, a concert at four and an open jam at five, the pattern the combos keep to this day. November meant the Percussive Arts Society convention in Indianapolis, the Percussion Ensemble played Glimmer, named for Ivan Treviño's piece, in ASU's newly renovated auditorium, and the Jazz Ensemble closed the semester in December with an evening of video game music, from Super Mario Bros. to Undertale.

2026 · The biggest year yet

From Big Spring to FiFi's to In C

After the Glass concerto in Big Spring in February came the Percussion Ensemble's House Music in April, a program of music for found objects built around John Cage's Living Room Music and Thierry De Mey's Table Music; Shuey and Friends with an ASU jazz combo at the Norris-Vincent College of Business; and in May both Let Freedom Ring!, ASU's outdoor concert for America's 250th with Jody Nix and the Texas Cowboys, and the night on bongos and mambo bell for Tito Puente Jr., which he calls the biggest gig of his life.

Then the 49th ASU Summer Music Camp, which he directed in June; band camp in August; timpani with the Amarillo Symphony in September; and the combos back at The House of FiFi DuBois in September and again for Halloween, in costume. In between came section percussion with the West Texas Symphony, a Jazz Ensemble tribute to Chick Corea in October, and on November 3 Terry Riley's In C, with ASU music faculty and students joining the Percussion Ensemble after Varèse's Ionisation and Mahler's Adagietto.

On the trail · Shuey Hikes

The other half of the story

The camper in the first photograph on this page never really went away. Trent's love of the outdoors comes from his dad, and Shuey Hikes is where the trips end up: canyon wades, alpine passes, sunrise campsites, and the long drives in between, filmed on a 360 camera. A June 2026 backpack with friends went from Crested Butte to Aspen over West Maroon Pass, and a 21-day solo road trip through nine states and five national parks, including a stop in Hermiston to sort old photos with his mom, is now becoming a twelve-part series.

It is the same curiosity he brings to the practice room, pointed at a trail map instead of a score.
